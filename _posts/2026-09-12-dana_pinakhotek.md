title:  "Dana Shutz’s Trouble and Appearance: sketching learnt helplessness"
---


The novelty in Dana Shutz’s solo exhibition at Pinakotek der Moderne finds itself in the wealth of sketches that open up the foyer to the exhibition in which we are able to see variations on the same motif: whereas some are initially sketched, developed in early prints and then fully fleshed out in oil paintings (Trouble and Appearance or Health) others are based on the end result and printed for production (Dear Painter). But as a whole Dana continues with her line of work, [as the author said to Bomb Magazine](https://bombmagazine.org/articles/2006/04/01/dana-schutz/) back in 2006: “I respond to what I think is happening in the world. The hypotheticals in the paintings can act as surrogates or narratives for phenomena that I feel are happening in culture”. Shutz positions herself as a of doctor of our times, however the treatment process seems to be cut short at the phase of diagnosis.

In *Health* we see a portrayal of the obsession with our outward self-presentation. A body-builder poses with disfigured proportions, viscera blowing out of an arm, blood and drugs infused into one’s body, a trainer applying the tap-cueing technique, and a background akin to a mechanics shop. The bikini top and the hair point to an ambiguity in the figure’s gender pointing to an homogenizing force behind the desire to alter one’s body for the others’ gaze. Here too, the sketch already has most elements of the composition in place, with the color and layering enabled by wet-on-wet painting adding to the grotesquerie. 

Even at different scales - where one could expect several layers portraying possibly contradictory elements in reality - we see a similar uni-dimensional diagnosis. Both *Catcher* and *The Hill* build compositions at a monumental scale that follow much the same process. On The *Catcher* resemblance to *Guernica* are clear both in the monochrome, the figures’ gestures, and the sky falling - the later representing mythological figures or belief systems, pointing to calamity and political collapse.

Contrasting in scope, *The Hill* is akin to a circus of artists trying to climb the ladder built on vomit, excrement and dirt. The critique of its own milieu is clear and concise - mirroring the process of diagnosis displayed in *Health* and *Mid-day.*


Art critic Peter Malone [puts the nail in the coffin](https://twocoatsofpaint.com/2023/12/dana-schutz-too-big-to-fail.html) by stating that “to parody media noise is not the same as addressing it, no matter how many variations are presented” whilst art historian Beniamino Foschini [offers a more insightful reading](https://www.frieze.com/article/must-see-dana-schutz-trouble-and-appearance-2026), recognizing that “Schutz treats painting, print and drawing as an act of cultural remembrance. In an art world where conviviality can mask collapse, gatherings risk becoming a measure of social currency rather than functioning as occasions for genuine exchange and discourse” - pointing at the often performative and void nature of said diagnostics whilst still being embedded in the very same structures they critic.

Now, the point is not to cast a moral judgement on a specific artist: one cannot exist in a system whilst being immune to its effects. A rather more fertile ground would be to recognize specifically *what*  Dana Shutz is trying to show us, what specific feeling and situation that is so contemporary, is at stake here - which I would argue *Trouble and Appearance* is about.

Both the title and painting capture the semantic proximity between “appearance” and “apparition”: the grand transparent figure, opened on a pose, is both an image of reality (possibly a fantasy of what it could be) and something that comes into view. The discontented worker reminds us of the general outburst that occurred when Extinction Rebellion activists blocked commuters on the London metro in 2019. Ideology and hope in grand display but ultimately dismissed by those it tries to gather to its cause. As individuals we experience both in an internal struggle.

What to do with this tension? Dana Shutz fixation with diagnosing and portraying the world ends up being more informative than the pieces themselves, hinting at a difficulty, or a learnt helplessness when faced with problems that the scope of one's actions, thereby forcing the viewer to understand their own place in this circus reality and address it via their means: who wouldn't desire to leave the stage of diagnosis and enter the realm of action and commitment?
